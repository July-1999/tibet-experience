My Unforgettable Tibet Travel Experience

By Sarah Mitchell · Updated June 2026 · 9 min read
🏔️ · Lhasa, Tibet

I had dreamed of visiting Tibet for years. The photographs I had seen — impossibly blue skies, whitewashed monasteries clinging to mountainsides, prayer flags dancing in the wind — had planted a seed that would not let go. When I finally stepped off the plane at Lhasa Gonggar Airport, nearly 3,600 meters above sea level, I took a deep breath and felt, for the first time, that I had arrived somewhere truly apart from the rest of the world.

"Tibet is not a destination you simply visit — it is a place that visits you in return, long after you have gone home."

First Impressions: Lhasa

Lhasa is the spiritual and cultural heart of Tibet. The first thing that struck me was the light — clear, crisp, and so bright that every color felt amplified. The white walls of the Potala Palace gleamed against a sky so blue it barely seemed real. Standing in the square below it, I felt incredibly small in the best possible way.

I spent my first full day exploring the Jokhang Temple, the most sacred Buddhist temple in Tibet. Pilgrims prostrated themselves on the stone plaza outside, their hands gliding over the worn surface worn smooth by centuries of devotion. Inside, the air was thick with the smell of yak butter and incense. Monks chanted in deep, resonant tones. I walked clockwise around the inner circuit, turning brass prayer wheels as I went, and felt a quiet I had not known I was missing.

That evening, I joined the evening kora (circumambulation) around the Barkhor Street. The atmosphere was electric — merchants selling turquoise jewelry and Tibetan carpets, pilgrims murmuring mantras, and the warm glow of butter lamps spilling from open temple doors. I sipped sweet yak butter tea from a tin cup and watched the sun sink behind the Potala. I had only been here one day, and I was already changed.

On the Road to Everest

After a few days of acclimatizing in Lhasa, my group set off on the road south toward Mount Everest — or Qomolangma as it is called in Tibetan, meaning "Goddess Mother of the World." The drive was a landscape in constant transformation: emerald valleys gave way to barren, moon-like plains, and then to the towering peaks of the Himalayas.

We passed through the Karo La Pass (5,045 m), where glacier-fed turquoise lakes sat beneath the remnants of the Noijin Kangsang glacier. At Gyantse, we visited the Kumbum Stupa, a nine-tiered jewel box of Tibetan Buddhist art with over 70 chapels. In Shigatse, the massive Tashilhunpo Monastery was a humbling reminder of the scale of Tibetan spirituality.

Everest Base Camp

Arriving at Everest Base Camp (5,200 m) was a moment I will never forget. The wind was fierce and the air was thin, but none of that mattered. There it was — the highest point on Earth, rising above the clouds, its summit catching the last light of day. I stood there, shivering and grinning, and thought: this is why I travel. For these moments of pure, overwhelming awe.

Namtso: The Heavenly Lake

Our final major stop was Namtso Lake (4,718 m), one of the highest saltwater lakes in the world. The journey there was rough — a bumpy dirt road that snaked across the open plateau — but the reward was worth every jolt. The lake's water was a shade of blue I have never seen anywhere else, deep and luminous, framed by the snowy peaks of the Nyenchen Tanglha range.

I walked along the shore, past small stone stupas built by pilgrims, and listened to the sound of the wind and water. A young Tibetan nomad offered me a ride on his horse for a small fee. I accepted, and we trotted along the shore in comfortable silence. He pointed at the mountains and said something in Tibetan. I did not understand the words, but I understood the feeling.

The People of Tibet

What I will remember most about Tibet is not the landscapes, as stunning as they were. It is the people. The old woman outside the Jokhang Temple who smiled at me with gold-toothed delight. The young monk at Sera Monastery who explained the art of debating with enthusiastic hand claps. The family in a roadside teahouse who insisted I share their bowl of tsampa and butter tea.

Tibetans have a warmth that transcends language barriers. They have endured a great deal, and yet their laughter is genuine, their hospitality boundless. Traveling through Tibet, I was reminded that the best journeys are not about checking off sights — they are about the connections we make along the way.

Practical Tips for First-Time Visitors

Altitude is real. Give yourself at least two full days in Lhasa before heading to higher elevations. Drink water constantly, avoid alcohol, and walk slowly. Most tour operators include acclimatization days for a reason — listen to your body.

Permits are mandatory. All foreign travelers need a Tibet Travel Permit, which can only be obtained through a registered travel agency. You will also need additional permits for Everest Base Camp and areas near the border. Do not attempt to travel independently — it is not permitted, and honestly, having a guide enriches the experience enormously.

Timing matters. May through October offers the most favorable weather. April, May, September, and early October are the sweet spots — warm days, clear skies, and fewer crowds than July and August.

Pack smart. Layers are essential. Thermals, a fleece, a windproof jacket, and a good sun hat cover most conditions. Sunscreen and lip balm are non-negotiable at high altitude. And bring an open mind — Tibet will challenge and reward you in equal measure.

The thin air, the vast skies, the ancient prayers carried on the wind — all of it conspires to pull you out of your head and into the moment.
